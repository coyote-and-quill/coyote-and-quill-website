Half your team is already using AI to do their jobs. Most of them haven't told you. They didn't wait for permission — they went to the closet. That's not a discipline problem. It's the gap, and it's costing you twice.

At home they use whatever works; at the office the good stuff is off-limits — so the people who've felt what AI can do route around the rules. A YC partner named this home-versus-work gap on the Lightcone podcast, but it doesn't sit still: it goes underground. Over half of workers now hide their AI use and pass the work off as their own.

That costs you twice. First, the risk you can't see: nearly half of workers admit to putting sensitive company data — customer records, internal docs, employee data — into public AI tools, and a breach that starts in shadow AI runs about $670,000 costlier. You cannot govern what you cannot see.

Second, the capability you're not capturing. Every closet user is quietly fixing their own friction — an hour saved here, a task automated there — and none of it aggregates, because it's off the books. It's worst at smaller companies, where unofficial use runs highest.

The reflex is to clamp down: no unapproved AI, use only the sanctioned model, one narrow lane. But restriction doesn't close the gap — it deepens it. A crackdown just pushes your most motivated people deeper into the closet.

Do the opposite. Bring them into the light — because the people already doing it are your R&D. You don't hand them a policy; you take what they built in the closet, turn it into a skill everyone can use, and spread it — one person's fix becomes the whole team's in a week.

Leadership's job shifts from originating to governing and spreading — and to owning what comes out. This isn't turning everyone loose; accountability stays at the top. When something breaks, you don't crack down — you fix the skill once, and because it's shared, it reaches everyone at once. Empowerment, with someone still holding the bag. That's readiness — the outcome of eight dimensions, not one of them: doctrine, organization, training, leadership, people, facilities, policy — and the tool everybody buys first.
