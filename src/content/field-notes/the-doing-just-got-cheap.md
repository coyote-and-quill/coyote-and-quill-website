A couple of days ago I watched a fireside chat with Greg Brockman — OpenAI's president — in front of a room full of founders.

He described the problem I'd just spent six months getting wrong.

[My launch note](/field-notes/the-integration-was-me) was about that mistake — I built an AI agent to run my day, then spent months quietly furious that it kept missing the obvious. The agent wasn't the problem. I was. I'd never taught it how I actually work — and an agent can't act on what you never say.

Here's Brockman saying the same thing from the other side of the table:

> "You have all these meetings, you didn't include the AI... you're asking it to help you with things and it has no information."

That's [him, around the nine-minute mark](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bBS93A0BeNI&t=534s). He calls it "a one-time shift that's happening now, which is really about context." Not a smarter model. Context. The exact gap I'd fallen into — the human never handed the machine what was in his own head.

It's a strange kind of reassurance to hear the person running the company that builds the frontier model tell a room of founders that the bottleneck isn't the model. It's you.

Then he went one step further, and this is the part worth sitting with.

> "Human attention is going to be this incredibly scarce resource. The doing of things now is easy. The 'Is this a good thing? Is this what I wanted? Is this aligned with my values, with my desires?' — that is going to become the single most important bottleneck."

Read that again if you run anything.

The *doing* is getting cheap — code, drafts, analysis, the work itself. What stays scarce is the human at the gate: Is this right? Is this what we wanted? Should this ship? For most of business history that judgment was a rounding error next to the cost of doing the work. That's inverting.

**The scarce, valuable thing is no longer the hands. It's the person deciding what's worth doing and standing behind it.**

That's not a threat to your job. It's a description of what your job becomes.

And it's why "just adopt AI" was always the wrong order. Adoption gives you the doing. The value is in wiring your judgment into the loop — so the machine handles the easy part and your scarce attention lands on the decisions that actually need a human. In regulated work, that isn't a nice-to-have; it's the law. Everywhere else, it's about to be the difference between a shop that scales and one that drowns in its own cheap output.

The tool was never the hard part. Neither is the model. It's you — and increasingly, that's the most valuable thing in the building.
